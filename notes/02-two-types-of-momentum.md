## The one-line definition

**Momentum** = what moved in a direction recently tends to keep moving that way for a while.

The opposite belief is **mean reversion**: what moved tends to snap back. This lecture is almost all momentum. Mean reversion shows up later only as a *short-term dip inside a longer uptrend*, or as the bounce after a crash.

## Time-series momentum (TS)

Look at **one** instrument against **its own past**.

Example: the 2-year Treasury future (TU) rose over the last 12 months. **Buy TU.** Hold about a month.

You do not care whether TU beat gold or stocks. You only care: *did this series go up?*

Consequences:

- You can be **net long the whole market**.
- A TS futures book is often just “trend-follow each contract.”

## Cross-sectional momentum (CS)

Look at **many** instruments against **each other**.

Example: AAPL was up 30% last year, BBRY up 10%. **Long AAPL, short BBRY** this year — even if both might fall, because you are betting AAPL **outperforms** BBRY.

Consequences:

- You are betting on **relative** winners, not on the market going up.
- A long–short CS book **hedges away a lot of market return**.
- It can still lose when the **shorts rebound** after a crash (file 10).

## Side-by-side

| | Time-series | Cross-sectional |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Did *this* go up? | Did *A beat B*? |
| Typical trade | Buy the thing that rose | Long the top rank, short the bottom rank |
| Market exposure | Often yes | Mostly hedged |
| Lecture examples | TU, VX, BR, HG, CL combo | 52 commodities; S&P 500 deciles |

## Exam trap

“Buy whatever went up” is **TS**.  
“Buy the best, short the worst” is **CS**.

A CS strategy can lose money in a bull market if the previous winners lag. A TS strategy can lose money in a choppy market even if some names are relative winners.

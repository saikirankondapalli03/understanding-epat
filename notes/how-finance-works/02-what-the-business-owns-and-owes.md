# 02 — What the business owns and owes

Every line on the balance sheet is a clue about *what kind of animal* this company is.

← [01 The two statements](01-the-two-statements.md) · Next: [03 Four ratio families](03-four-ratio-families.md) →

---

## In one sentence

Assets are the company. Liabilities and equity are the two ways those assets were paid for.

---

## Assets: the company itself

Desai’s ice-cream example is the right mental model. Häagen-Dazs owns:

- the ice cream waiting to be sold, plus the chocolate, dulce de leche, and coffee beans that will become ice cream (**inventory**: raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods)
- the factory and the trucks that move it (**PP&E**)
- maybe cash sitting in the bank
- maybe money customers have not paid yet (**receivables**)

That pile *is* the business. Different industries build very different piles.

### Cash and marketable securities

Cash is obvious. **Marketable securities** are short-term investments (often government bills) that can be sold almost immediately. Analysts usually lump them with cash.

Huge cash piles look strange at first. Why would a living company sit on a mountain of money? Three common reasons:

1. **Insurance** — a buffer for bad times
2. **War chest** — dry powder for acquisitions
3. **No good projects** — management cannot find investments that earn enough, so cash piles up

Cash earns little. Holding too much is not “safe and smart” by default. It can mean the company is cautious, opportunistic, or stuck. US firms as a group have at times sat on trillions of dollars of cash; Apple alone has held on the order of **$250 billion**. That is not a giant checking account. Because idle cash earns almost nothing, firms park it in **marketable securities** — usually government paper that can be sold tomorrow.

Young tech firms that just went public often look cash-rich: they raised money and have not spent it yet. Facebook in 2013 is that picture in the matching game.

### Accounts receivable

This is “customers who already bought, but have not paid.”

**Rule of thumb:**

- Sell to **consumers** (groceries, restaurants, airlines tickets) → receivables stay small; people pay at the till or by card.
- Sell to **other companies** (chips to laptop makers, parcels to corporate shippers) → receivables swell; 30–90 day invoices are normal.

**Reflection — Walmart, Staples, Intel (2016 idea):** who has receivables as a bigger slice of sales?

- Walmart, almost all consumers at the till: receivables tiny (~1% of sales).
- Staples, mix of walk-in consumers and office accounts: in the middle (~7% of sales).
- Intel, selling chips to other companies: highest (~9% of sales).

Companies that sell to companies *extend credit*. That is the whole B2B vs B2C split.

Follow-on reflection: **whose payable is Intel’s receivable?** A PC maker such as Dell or Lenovo. One firm’s asset is the other firm’s bill. Always ask who the customer is.

If receivables are *most* of the assets, you are probably not looking at a grocer. You may be looking at a **bank**. A bank’s “receivables” are loans. Your mortgage is the bank’s asset. Your deposit is the bank’s liability. The balance sheet is a mirror of yours.

### Inventory

Goods waiting to be sold: raw materials, work in progress, finished products.

- **Zero inventory** usually means a **service** business: software, social networks, airlines (they sell seats, not planes), banks, a law firm, an advertising agency, a medical practice. They have nothing sitting on a shelf to sell.
- **Huge, slow inventory** can mean books, jewelry, or anything that sits on a shelf.
- **Tiny, fast inventory** can mean restaurants or a made-to-order manufacturer.

Finance people treat inventory as **risk that must be financed**. Fashion inventory can become worthless after one bad season. Iron ore can still be sold on a spot market. That is why some analysts ignore inventory when they test short-term safety (the quick ratio, next file).

### Property, plant, and equipment (PP&E)

The heavy stuff: stores, dams, planes, factories, warehouses.

High PP&E: utilities (think hydroelectric dams), airlines, grocery cold-chains, parcel networks.  
Low PP&E: software, online retail (relative to a supermarket), drug companies that outsource plants, social networks.

An airline’s planes are PP&E, not inventory. The company is not in the business of selling aircraft; it sells transportation.

### Other assets, intangibles, goodwill

“Other assets” is often code for **intangibles**: patents, licenses, brands, and **goodwill**.

**Goodwill** appears when one company buys another for more than the accounting book value of the target. The extra is parked as goodwill.

Example, named: in 2016 Microsoft paid about **$26 billion** for LinkedIn, whose recorded assets were about **$7 billion**. The extra **$19 billion** became other assets / goodwill. What was actually bought? Not desks. Data and relationships — on the order of hundreds of millions of members — that accounting had never put a number on, but that could help Microsoft sell enterprise software. Conservatism hid the asset until a buyer wrote a cheque.

**Gilead-style twist (chapter quiz):** a patent a drug firm *developed itself* for a hugely profitable treatment may still be missing from the balance sheet. Google’s headquarters, Ford’s dealer receivables, and Facebook’s bank cash are easy for accountants to record. An internally grown patent is not. Finance people find this infuriating, and also useful: a fat “other assets” line often means “this firm has been on an acquisition spree in an intangible-heavy industry.”

## Liabilities and equity: who funded the pile

There are only two ultimate sources of money: **lenders** and **owners**.

### Accounts payable

Money owed to suppliers. One firm’s payable is another firm’s receivable.

A giant retailer with a strong hand over suppliers can take a long time to pay. That looks like high payables. It is not always distress. Check cash: if cash is also fat, the company is using supplier credit as cheap financing, not drowning.

A weak company may have high payables because it *cannot* pay. Same number, opposite story. Always look for a second clue.

### Notes payable

Short-term borrowed money, often from banks. A huge notes-payable line plus enormous receivables is a classic **bank** shape: deposits (and short-term funding) on the right, loans on the left.

### Accrued items

Work already received, cash not yet paid. Salaries in the middle of a pay cycle are the textbook case.

### Long-term debt

Borrowed money with an **explicit interest rate**. That is the difference between debt and most other liabilities. Accounts payable do not usually charge a coupon. A bond does.

Student loans and mortgages are your version of this line.

**Net debt** is a useful twist: treat cash as negative debt.

\[
\text{Net debt} \approx \text{debt} - \text{cash}
\]

A firm with 30% debt and 20% cash is less dangerous than a firm with 25% debt and 5% cash. The second one has less of a cushion.

Desai’s matching-game version of this: company E had more headline debt than company I, but also far more cash. Treat cash as negative debt and **I is the riskier borrower**. Never read a debt ratio without glancing at the cash line.

### Shareholders’ equity

Owners put money in. Profits that were not paid out as dividends stay in as **retained earnings**. Both live in equity.

Equity is a **residual** claim:

- Owners get the upside if the firm booms (variable return, ownership rights).
- Debt gets a **fixed** return (interest) and is paid **first** in bankruptcy.
- Owners stand last and can be left with nothing.

Typical synonyms in this chapter: shareholders’ equity, net worth, owners’ equity, common stock. They are trying to name the same leftover.

Retained earnings are not a vault of cash. They are the running total of profits not paid out. Accounting treats them as if the owners had received a dividend and immediately put it back in — the same economic act as the original investment.

### Preferred stock

A hybrid, used when a company needs money in shaky times (or when venture investors want extra protection).

| If things go badly | If things go well |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| Preferred is paid **before** common shareholders | Preferred can often **convert** into common and ride the upside, unlike pure debt |
| Still paid **after** true lenders | Dividends can be received even when common gets nothing |

It is called preferred because it is preferred. It is called a hybrid because it is neither clean debt nor clean equity.

**Why issue it?** Imagine a shaky company. Common stock looks like a coin flip. Straight debt may not pay you enough for the risk. Preferred can be the only security that gets the deal done.

**Why venture capitalists take it almost always:** protection if the startup fails, conversion if it becomes a unicorn.

**Chapter quiz story:** in 2009 Warren Buffett put billions into Dow Chemical via preferred. Advantages: paid before common in bankruptcy, possible dividends when common gets none, still an ownership-like claim. “Dividends must be even-numbered percentages (2%, 4%, …)” is **not** an advantage. That was the trap option.

Only a distressed or special-situation company in a mixed industry sample will typically show preferred stock. In Desai’s game, that fingerprint helped flag a struggling bookseller.

## Capital structure

The mix of debt vs equity is **capital structure**. Chapter 4 goes deeper. For now:

- Heavy debt can magnify owner returns — and owner losses.
- Stable businesses (utilities, some consumer staples) can carry more debt.
- High-wire technology businesses usually should not.

You already know this from housing. A mortgage lets you control a home you could not buy with cash. If prices rise, your percentage return explodes. If prices fall, you can be wiped out.

## Detective habits

When you open any balance sheet, hunt extremes:

| Extreme | Often means |
|---------|-------------|
| Cash > 50% of assets | Young tech, or a firm hoarding after a raise / before deals |
| Receivables huge, inventory zero | Bank, or a pure service firm selling on invoice |
| Inventory huge and slow | Books, fashion, anything that sits |
| PP&E > 60% | Utility, airline, heavy network |
| Other assets huge | Acquisitions + intangibles (pharma, software roll-ups) |
| Payables huge *and* cash healthy | Power over suppliers |
| Preferred stock present | Stress, or a special financing story |
| Equity tiny, assets huge | High leverage — banks live here |

Do not identify a company from one line. Stack two or three extremes until the story gets boringly obvious.

## Check yourself

1. Why does a restaurant have almost no receivables, while a chipmaker has plenty?
2. Are an airline’s planes inventory or PP&E? Why?
3. Microsoft buys a company for far more than its book value. Where does the extra typically show up?
4. High payables plus high cash: distress or strength? How do you decide?
5. Intel’s receivables are whose payables?
6. Two firms have similar debt/assets. One is stuffed with cash. Who is safer to lend more to?

**Answers:** (1) Diners pay now; other companies pay later on invoices. (2) PP&E — the airline uses planes, it does not sell them as its product. (3) Other assets / goodwill. (4) Likely strength (supplier credit); confirm with cash, profits, and whether payables are growing because suppliers are being stretched or because volume is growing. (5) Its business customers — PC makers such as Dell or Lenovo. (6) The cash-rich one; think in net debt.

## Step 1 — Service firms (inventory = 0)

Four columns have no inventory: the book’s E, G, M, N.

Four names fit: UPS, Facebook, Southwest, Citigroup.

An airline is a service even though it owns planes. Those planes are **PP&E**, not merchandise. The product is a seat, not an aircraft.

### N is the bank (Citigroup)

The outlier of the whole table.

- Receivables are most of the assets; the collection “period” is measured in **years** (on the order of twenty), not days.
- Notes payable are huge.
- Leverage is the highest in the set (assets many times equity).
- Almost no PP&E, no inventory.

**Mirror:** your mortgage is the bank’s asset; your deposit is the bank’s liability (notes payable / deposits).

**What it is like to run a bank.** Banks run a **spread**: they charge more on loans than they pay on deposits. They take short-term money and lend it long-term. That **maturity transformation** is why they are valuable and why they fail.

**Crisis spiral (do not skip this story):** doubts about loan quality → depositors pull money → the bank must sell loans in a hurry → loan prices fall → more doubts → more outflows. High leverage leaves little room for error. Nearly every financial crisis has this shape.

### E and M are the two transportation firms

Both have heavy PP&E. Facebook does not.

Split them with **who pays**:

| Clue | Southwest (airline) | UPS (parcel) |
|------|---------------------|--------------|
| Customer | You and I, paying now | Other companies, on invoice |
| Collection | A handful of days | About a month and a half |
| Other liabilities | Budget airline, light on old pensions | Large **defined-benefit** pension (UPS was once employee-owned; one of the world’s large DB plans) |

So: **M = Southwest**, **E = UPS**. The pension line is the confirming clue, not trivia.

### G is Facebook

What remains. In 2013 it was the youngest firm on the list and had recently gone public.

- Cash more than half of assets
- Equity the largest share in the service group (around four-fifths)
- Little PP&E, no inventory

**Why equity looks so fat on a young firm:** balance-sheet equity is recorded at the moment money comes in. A recent IPO writes a big number that has not yet been grown through years of operations. Conservatism / historical cost again.

**The sequel the chapter wants you to see:** as Facebook matured it spent the cash pile on WhatsApp and Instagram. Cash down, **goodwill / other assets** up. WhatsApp (2014) was bought for about **$19 billion** against a book value of about **$51 million**. That gap became goodwill — the same Microsoft–LinkedIn logic.

## Step 2 — True retailers (customers pay now)

Fast collection (about a week, not a month) plus real inventory: A, B, H, I, K.

Five names: Amazon, Barnes & Noble, Kroger, Walgreens, Yum!.

**Nordstrom is not in this group.** Its own-brand charge card makes customers slow to pay. It behaves like a bit of a bank. Park it with the leftovers.

Retail is a game of **moving inventory**. Sort by speed.

### H = Yum! (restaurant)

Inventory turns on the order of **thirty times a year** (~eleven days on the shelf). You want the chicken gone, not ageing. Highest ROE in the retailer group, and a lot of it is leverage plus fast turns on thin-looking product that still makes money at the store.

### B = Barnes & Noble (bookstore)

The other extreme: inventory that sits on the order of **a hundred days**. Books age slowly, and they do not fly off the shelf. Extra fingerprints:

- Losing money (negative margin) — bookshops under Amazon
- The **only preferred stock** in the whole game — a shaky firm using a hybrid security

### A = Amazon (online retailer)

Of the three left, A has the **least PP&E**. Two brick-and-mortar names remain (Kroger, Walgreens), so the online one should look lighter in stores and warehouses than a grocer.

Confirming clues, stacked:

- Profit ~0, which was Amazon’s public reputation in that era
- Very high **accounts payable**
- Very high **cash**, so the payables are power over suppliers, not bounced cheques

### I = Kroger, K = Walgreens

Both are physical retail. Split with equipment and who pays.

- **I has more PP&E.** Grocery is a **cold chain**: refrigeration, bigger boxes, more kit. A drugstore is shelves and a pharmacy counter.
- **I collects even faster** (almost all cash/card at the till). A drugstore takes a slice of revenue from **insurers**, so it is a little more B2B.
- **I turns inventory faster** (perishable food vs a mix of toothpaste and pills).

So **I = Kroger**, **K = Walgreens**.

## Step 3 — The leftovers

Five names left: Microsoft, Nordstrom, Duke Energy, Pfizer, Dell.

Columns left in the book: C, D, F, J, L.

### First cut: who owns heavy plant?

C, D, F have almost no PP&E. J and L have a lot.

Dell, Pfizer, and Microsoft do not pour concrete for a living. Duke (power plants) and Nordstrom (stores) do.

### L = Duke Energy, J = Nordstrom

Split with **inventory**:

- Electricity **cannot be stored** in a warehouse. Utility inventory is tiny.
- A department store is clothes on racks.

Confirming clues:

- **L’s EBITDA margin is fat relative to net margin** — depreciation on plants. Utility people talk EBITDA on purpose.
- **J collects slowly** for a retailer — the private-label **charge card**. Receivables linger like a bank’s. Debt is also meaningful.

### C = Dell

Of C, D, F, only C has **thin** margins. PCs became a commodity over the prior decade. Software and branded drugs did not.

Confirming clue: inventory held on the order of **ten days**. Dell’s model was **just-in-time**: take the order, then build, manufacturing largely outsourced, more than half of sales to businesses (so receivables look B2B, ~two months). Low PP&E because someone else owns the factory.

### D = Pfizer, F = Microsoft

The last two look alike: fat net margins (~25%) and fatter EBITDA margins (~40%+).

Split with **other assets** and **pensions** and **cash**:

| | Pfizer (D) | Microsoft (F) |
|--|------------|---------------|
| Other assets | Huge — years of buying other drug firms (Pharmacia, Wyeth, Hospira in the chapter’s telling) | Smaller |
| Other liabilities | Old **defined-benefit** pension | Younger firm, **defined-contribution** plan |
| Cash | High, but not the record | The cash mountain of the pair |
| Interest coverage | Fine | Enormous — almost no interest burden |

Intangible-heavy industry + consolidation → goodwill. That is D.

## Pocket glossary

| Term | Meaning in this chapter |
|------|-------------------------|
| **Asset** | What the firm owns |
| **Liability** | What it owes to non-owners |
| **Equity / net worth** | Owners’ residual claim; assets minus liabilities |
| **Current** | Due to become cash, or due to be paid, within a year |
| **PP&E** | Property, plant, and equipment — the heavy tools |
| **Receivable** | Customer still owes you |
| **Payable** | You still owe a supplier |
| **Goodwill** | Extra paid in an acquisition above book value |
| **Conservatism** | Accounting’s habit of under-recording fuzzy assets |
| **Capital structure** | Mix of debt and equity |
| **Preferred stock** | Hybrid: paid before common, can share in upside |
| **EBIT** | Operating profit (earnings before interest and tax) |
| **EBITDA** | EBIT plus depreciation and amortization — a cash-ish cousin |
| **ROE** | Net profit / equity — return to owners |
| **ROA** | Net profit / assets |
| **ROC / ROIC** | Operating return on debt + equity; less polluted by leverage |
| **Current ratio** | Current assets / current liabilities |
| **Quick ratio** | (Current assets − inventory) / current liabilities |
| **Asset turnover** | Sales / assets |
| **Inventory days** | 365 / inventory turnover |
| **Collection period** | Days to get paid |
| **Interest coverage** | EBIT / interest |
| **DuPont** | ROE = margin × turnover × leverage |
| **Gross margin** | Gross profit / revenue — juice in the product before overhead |
| **Capitalization** | Debt + equity; the financial claims, not payables |
| **Net debt** | Debt minus cash (cash as negative debt) |
| **EBIAT** | EBIT after tax; numerator of ROC |
| **Times interest earned** | Another name for interest coverage |
| **Payable period** | Days you take to pay suppliers |
| **Working capital** | Current assets minus current liabilities; inventory + receivables − payables in spirit |
| **Spread (banks)** | Charge more on loans than you pay on deposits |
| **Maturity transformation** | Banks turn short-term deposits into long-term loans |
| **LBO** | Leveraged buyout — load the firm with debt to buy out shareholders |
| **Defined-benefit pension** | Firm promises a retirement cheque (UPS, old pharma) — a fat “other liability” |

## Recap in twelve lines

1. A number without a denominator is trivia.
2. Balance sheet = snapshot; income statement = year’s movie.
3. Assets = liabilities + equity, always.
4. Scale to 100 so shapes can be compared.
5. Hunt extremes, then confirm with a second clue.
6. Zero inventory → service. Fast collections → consumer. Slow collections → companies (or a bank).
7. Banks are mirrors. Your loan is their asset.
8. Liquidity is survival. Profit is the engine. Leverage is a multiplier. Turnover is sweat from assets.
9. Leverage magnifies gains and losses. That is the whole catch.
10. Do not stack financial risk on business risk.
11. ROE is famous and impure. Split it. Prefer ROC when leverage is doing the talking.
12. The best analysis is a story: one firm, several years, versus its industry.

## Quiz — every end-of-chapter theme

These cover the book’s ten closing questions plus the extra stories in the chapter.

**1.** Extra leverage can lift ROE. What is the problem?

- A. It reduces productivity, which must lower ROE
- B. Leverage profits are ignored because they are not cash
- C. It multiplies losses too and raises risk
- D. There is nothing bad about using other people’s money

**2.** Who can usually carry high leverage more safely?

- A. High-growth firms in new industries
- B. Stable, predictable industries with reliable cash flows
- C. Technology companies as a class
- D. Firms with low profitability

**3.** An investor buys preferred stock in a chemical company. Which is **not** an advantage of preferred?

- A. Paid before common in bankruptcy
- B. May get dividends even when common gets none
- C. Still associated with ownership, unlike debt
- D. Dividends must be even-numbered percentages (2%, 4%, …)

**4.** Which is *least* likely to appear as an asset?

- A. A drug firm’s internally developed, hugely profitable patent
- B. A tech firm’s headquarters building
- C. Money dealers owe a car company for vehicles shipped
- D. Tens of billions sitting in a social network’s bank accounts

**5.** Highest inventory turnover?

- A. A sandwich / fast-food chain
- B. A bookstore chain
- C. A grocery chain
- D. An airline

**6.** A distinguishing feature of many **retailers**:

- A. High ROE
- B. Low receivables collection period
- C. High inventory turnover (always)
- D. High debt / assets

**7.** A giant miner shows receivables as a large slice of assets. Who most likely **owes** that miner?

- A. A global bank
- B. A mining recruitment agency
- C. A food distributor
- D. A steel manufacturer

**8.** Who cares most about the current ratio?

- A. Stockholders
- B. Suppliers
- C. Competitors
- D. Customers

**9.** True or false: a high ROE is always a good thing.

**10.** A home-improvement retailer issues bonds. Main difference vs accounts payable?

- A. Debt carries an explicit interest rate
- B. Debt is ownership
- C. Debt is a residual claim
- D. Debt is owed only to suppliers

**11.** Fill in: ROE = ________ × ________ × ________

**12.** EBITDA margin ≫ net margin, PP&E huge. Software or electric utility?

**13.** Collection period of several *thousand* days. First hypothesis?

**14.** Why might payables *fall* when a company is in crisis?

**15.** High cash + high payables at an online retailer. Distress or power?

**16.** Name the three reasons a collection period can look “too long.”

**17.** Why did large pharma’s leverage drift up over two decades (Merck / Pfizer story)?

**18.** What did Microsoft actually buy, above LinkedIn’s book value?

## Mini cases to talk through out loud

**A.** Two coffee chains. Same sales. Chain 1 rents everything. Chain 2 owns its stores. Who has more PP&E? Who likely has higher asset turnover? Who has more depreciation, so a bigger gap between EBIT and EBITDA?

**B.** You are a supplier to a customer whose current ratio is 0.7. Before you refuse to ship, what else do you want to know?

**C.** Management brags that ROE beat the industry. Margin and turnover lagged. Leverage was double the industry. Write the one-sentence performance review.

**D.** A chip firm and a power company have the same ROE. Sketch their likely DuPont profiles.

**E.** Burberry vs Rio Tinto: which ratio do you trust more for liquidity, and why?

**F.** Walk the bank crisis spiral in four steps.

## Answers

1. **C** — leverage multiplies losses too.  
2. **B**  
3. **D** — even-numbered coupons are not a feature of preferred.  
4. **A** — internally built intangibles are often missing (conservatism).  
5. **A** — sandwiches turn faster than groceries; bookstores are slow; airlines are not merchandise businesses.  
6. **B** — consumers pay now. Inventory turns vary a lot *among* retailers.  
7. **D** — steel mills buy the ore; banks, recruiters, and grocers do not.  
8. **B**  
9. **False** — it can be debt in costume.  
10. **A**  
11. **margin × asset turnover × leverage**  
12. **Electric utility**  
13. **A bank**  
14. **Suppliers stop offering credit** (cash on delivery).  
15. **Power, if cash is healthy.**  
16. Slack collection; generous credit to juice sales; hidden bad debts.  
17. Cash flows got steadier as they bought biotech science instead of living the riskiest R&D themselves; lenders allowed more debt.  
18. User data / network (and similar unrecorded intangibles), parked as goodwill.

**Mini cases, sketches:**

- **A.** Chain 2 has more PP&E, probably lower turnover, more depreciation, fatter EBITDA vs EBIT.  
- **B.** How fast does inventory become cash? Is this a grocer or a slow manufacturer? Is cash growing? Is the ratio always low in this industry?  
- **C.** “You matched owner returns by putting more risk on the owners, while running a weaker operation than peers.”  
- **D.** Chip: high margin, modest turnover, low leverage. Power: middling margin, low turnover, high leverage.  
- **E.** Trust Burberry’s *quick* ratio; coats have no spot market.  
- **F.** Asset-quality scare → deposit outflow → fire-sale of loans → lower prices → worse scare.

## How to keep this alive until Chapter 2

Pick one public company you already like. Print (or screenshot) two years of:

- a common-size balance sheet
- profit margin, asset turnover, leverage, ROE
- interest coverage
- inventory days and collection days

Write a half-page story. If you cannot write the story, you do not yet own the tools.

Desai’s own closing assignments: teach the unidentified-industries game to someone else; then open a real company’s accounts — yours, or a favourite — with the same tools.

Then open Chapter 2, where cash and the future take over from these historical accounts.

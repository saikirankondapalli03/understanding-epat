## Pocket glossary

| Term | Meaning in this chapter |
|------|-------------------------|
| **Asset** | What the firm owns |
| **Liability** | What it owes to non-owners |
| **Equity / net worth** | Owners’ residual claim; assets minus liabilities |
| **Current** | Due to become cash, or due to be paid, within a year |
| **PP&E** | Property, plant, and equipment — the heavy tools |
| **Receivable** | Customer still owes you |
| **Payable** | You still owe a supplier |
| **Goodwill** | Extra paid in an acquisition above book value |
| **Conservatism** | Accounting’s habit of under-recording fuzzy assets |
| **Capital structure** | Mix of debt and equity |
| **Preferred stock** | Hybrid: paid before common, can share in upside |
| **EBIT** | Operating profit (earnings before interest and tax) |
| **EBITDA** | EBIT plus depreciation and amortization — a cash-ish cousin |
| **ROE** | Net profit / equity — return to owners |
| **ROA** | Net profit / assets |
| **ROC / ROIC** | Operating return on debt + equity; less polluted by leverage |
| **Current ratio** | Current assets / current liabilities |
| **Quick ratio** | (Current assets − inventory) / current liabilities |
| **Asset turnover** | Sales / assets |
| **Inventory days** | 365 / inventory turnover |
| **Collection period** | Days to get paid |
| **Interest coverage** | EBIT / interest |
| **DuPont** | ROE = margin × turnover × leverage |
| **Working capital** | Current assets minus current liabilities; the cash tied in day-to-day operations |

## Recap in twelve lines

1. A number without a denominator is trivia.
2. Balance sheet = snapshot; income statement = year’s movie.
3. Assets = liabilities + equity, always.
4. Scale to 100 so shapes can be compared.
5. Hunt extremes, then confirm with a second clue.
6. Zero inventory → service. Fast collections → consumer. Slow collections → companies (or a bank).
7. Banks are mirrors. Your loan is their asset.
8. Liquidity is survival. Profit is the engine. Leverage is a multiplier. Turnover is sweat from assets.
9. Leverage magnifies gains and losses. That is the whole catch.
10. Do not stack financial risk on business risk.
11. ROE is famous and impure. Split it. Prefer ROC when leverage is doing the talking.
12. The best analysis is a story: one firm, several years, versus its industry.

## Quiz (original questions, same ideas as the chapter)

**1.** What is the main problem with leverage?

- A. It always reduces ROE
- B. It also magnifies losses and adds risk
- C. Interest is not a cash cost
- D. Lenders become owners

**2.** Which firms can usually carry more debt safely?

- A. Early-stage tech with a new product
- B. Stable, predictable cash-flow businesses
- C. Firms with negative interest coverage
- D. Firms with the highest inventory days

**3.** A patent developed inside the company is often missing from the balance sheet. Why?

- A. Patents are illegal to record
- B. Conservatism: internally built intangibles are hard to value precisely
- C. Only American firms record patents
- D. Patents are liabilities

**4.** Who typically cares most about the current ratio?

- A. Customers deciding on color
- B. Competitors’ marketing teams
- C. Suppliers wondering if they will be paid
- D. The tax authority

**5.** Which business should show the fastest inventory turnover?

- A. A bookstore
- B. A sandwich chain
- C. A furniture maker with handmade pieces
- D. A wine collector’s shop

**6.** A short receivables collection period is a hallmark of many:

- A. Retailers selling to consumers
- B. Chipmakers selling to PC firms
- C. Banks
- D. Utilities billing other utilities only

**7.** True or false: a high ROE is always good.

**8.** Home Depot issues bonds. What is the main difference between that debt and accounts payable?

- A. Debt is ownership
- B. Debt is a residual claim
- C. Debt carries an explicit interest rate
- D. Payables never have to be paid

**9.** A mining firm’s receivables are 20% of assets. Who is the more likely customer sitting in that receivable?

- A. A retail bank
- B. A steel manufacturer
- C. A recruiting agency
- D. A grocery distributor

**10.** Preferred stock is attractive to a cautious investor because:

- A. It is paid before common in trouble, but can still participate if things go well
- B. It is paid before the tax authority
- C. Dividends must be even percentages
- D. It never converts

**11.** Fill in: ROE = ________ × ________ × ________

**12.** A firm’s EBITDA margin is much higher than its net margin, and PP&E is huge. Which industry is more plausible: software or electric utility?

**13.** Collection period of several *thousand* days. First hypothesis?

**14.** Why might payables *fall* when a company is in crisis?

**15.** Name one reason a high-cash, high-payable online retailer is not necessarily going bankrupt.

## Mini cases to talk through out loud

**A.** Two coffee chains. Same sales. Chain 1 rents everything. Chain 2 owns its stores. Who has more PP&E? Who likely has higher asset turnover? Who has more depreciation, so a bigger gap between EBIT and EBITDA?

**B.** You are a supplier to a customer whose current ratio is 0.7. Before you refuse to ship, what else do you want to know?

**C.** Management brags that ROE beat the industry. Margin and turnover lagged. Leverage was double the industry. Write the one-sentence performance review.

**D.** A chip firm and a power company have the same ROE. Sketch their likely DuPont profiles.

---

## Answers

1. **B** — leverage multiplies losses too.  
2. **B**  
3. **B**  
4. **C**  
5. **B**  
6. **A**  
7. **False** — it can be debt in costume.  
8. **C**  
9. **B** — the miner sells to firms that use metal.  
10. **A**  
11. **margin × asset turnover × leverage** (profit/sales × sales/assets × assets/equity)  
12. **Electric utility** (heavy plant, big depreciation).  
13. **A bank** (those “receivables” are loans).  
14. **Suppliers stop offering credit** and demand cash on delivery.  
15. **Cash is healthy**, so stretched payables are bargaining power, not bounced checks.

**Mini cases, sketches:**

- **A.** Chain 2 has more PP&E, probably lower turnover, more depreciation, fatter EBITDA vs EBIT.  
- **B.** How fast does inventory become cash? Is this a grocer or a slow manufacturer? Is cash growing? Is the ratio always low in this industry?  
- **C.** “You matched owner returns by putting more risk on the owners, while running a weaker operation than peers.”  
- **D.** Chip: high margin, modest turnover, low leverage. Power: middling margin, low turnover, high leverage.

## How to keep this alive until Chapter 2

Pick one public company you already like. Print (or screenshot) two years of:

- a common-size balance sheet
- profit margin, asset turnover, leverage, ROE
- interest coverage
- inventory days and collection days

Write a half-page story. If you cannot write the story, you do not yet own the tools. Then open Chapter 2, where Desai will tell you why cash and the future matter more than these historical accounts.

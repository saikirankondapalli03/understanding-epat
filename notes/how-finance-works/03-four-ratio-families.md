# 03 — Four questions, four ratio families

Ratios exist because a raw number has no plot.

← [02 What the business owns and owes](02-what-the-business-owns-and-owes.md) · Next: [04 Industry fingerprints](04-industry-fingerprints.md) →

---

## In one sentence

Every useful ratio in Chapter 1 answers one of four questions: can it pay its bills soon, does it make money, how did it fund itself, and how hard do its assets work?

---

## Why divide one number by another

“Coca-Cola earned billions.” Is that a lot? You cannot know.

“Coca-Cola kept about 16 cents of every sales dollar.” Now you know something. You can compare that 16% to last year, to Pepsi, and to a grocer who is lucky to keep 3 cents.

Desai’s four questions:

1. **Liquidity** — can this firm survive the next year without running out of cash?
2. **Profitability** — does the business keep money, and what do owners earn on their stake?
3. **Financing / leverage** — how much of the firm is other people’s money?
4. **Productivity / efficiency** — how much activity is squeezed out of the asset pile?

---

## 1. Liquidity — will the lights stay on?

Most bankruptcies are cash deaths, not “the brand became unpopular overnight.”

### Current ratio

\[
\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{current assets}}{\text{current liabilities}}
\]

Question: if we had to settle short-term bills over the next six to twelve months — including suppliers — would short-term assets cover them? It is also the question a supplier asks before shipping more goods.

Suppliers care about this number more than shareholders do. A current ratio below 1 means current bills exceed current assets. That can be fine for a grocer who turns inventory into cash every few days. It is terrifying for a slow manufacturer.

### Quick ratio (acid test)

\[
\text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{current assets} - \text{inventory}}{\text{current liabilities}}
\]

Same idea, but inventory is treated as suspect. Fashion leftover may be unsellable. Iron ore is not.

The book’s table also prints a close cousin:

\[
\frac{\text{cash + marketable securities + accounts receivable}}{\text{current liabilities}}
\]

That is the same skeptical spirit: count only the assets that are already cash or very near cash. Inventory and miscellaneous current items are left out.

**Who wants which ratio?**

- Luxury fashion (Burberry): prefer the *quick* ratio. There is no spot market for last season’s coats. A style mistake can make inventory unsellable even on sale.
- A miner (Rio Tinto) with a liquid commodity: current ratio is less misleading. Ore can be sold.
- A steel mini-mill (Nucor) sits in between.

**BlackBerry, 2013:** the Z10 smartphone arrived late in a market where products rot on the shelf. The firm had to treat on the order of **$1 billion** of inventory as worth ~zero. That is why finance people say inventory is *risk that must be financed*, not just “operations.”

**The trade-off:** extra liquidity is safety, and safety earns a low return. A pile of cash does not go bankrupt. It also does not build the next factory.

## 2. Profitability — is the engine making money?

Always compare profit to a base.

### Profit margin

\[
\text{Profit margin} = \frac{\text{net profit}}{\text{revenue}}
\]

“Per sales dollar, how many cents survive?”

Grocers: tiny margins, huge volume.  
Software and patents: fat margins. Turning sand into chips, or code into licenses, adds more value than moving cereal boxes.

### Return on equity (ROE)

\[
\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{net profit}}{\text{shareholders’ equity}}
\]

“Per dollar the owners left in the business, what did they earn this year?”

This is the celebrity ratio. Managers are judged on it. It is also easy to fake with debt. File 05 (DuPont) is built to stop you from being fooled.

### Return on assets (ROA)

\[
\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{net profit}}{\text{total assets}}
\]

“How much profit did the whole asset pile produce?”  
This ignores how the pile was financed, so it is cleaner operationally than ROE, but still uses net profit (which is after interest).

### EBITDA margin

\[
\text{EBITDA margin} = \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{revenue}}
\]

Useful when depreciation is enormous (utilities, cable networks, heavy plant). Net profit can look modest while cash generation is strong, because the accountant is spreading a long-lived factory across decades.

In the matching game, one column keeps about 9 cents of net profit per sales dollar but about **28 cents of EBITDA**. That gap is a utility’s depreciation, not a miracle. Another column keeps about **45 cents of EBITDA** per sales dollar — a patent/software machine.

**A utility fingerprint:** ordinary profit margin, *fat* EBITDA margin. The gap is depreciation.

Chapter 1 already points at Amazon: little net profit, serious EBITDA. File 04b uses that as a confirming clue for the online retailer.

## 3. Leverage — other people’s money

A lever lets you move a rock you should not be able to move. Debt lets owners control assets they could not buy with their own cash.

### House example (learn this cold)

| | All cash | With a mortgage |
|--|----------|-----------------|
| House | 100 | 500 |
| Debt | 0 | 400 |
| Your equity | 100 | 100 |

You are **not richer** in the second house. Equity is still 100. You just control a bigger asset.

If the house rises 10%:

- all cash: you make 10
- mortgaged: house is 550, debt still 400, equity is 150 → you made **50%**

If the house falls 20%:

- all cash: you lose 20%
- mortgaged: house is 400, debt 400, equity **0** → you lost 100%

Leverage multiplies wins *and* losses. That is what is “bad” about it. Nothing else.

You are not richer in the mortgaged house. Some people think you are, because you live in more square metres. Some think the all-cash buyer is richer because they owe nothing. **Wealth is the equity line.** Both cases start at 100.

### Real-world perspective — private equity (Alan Jones)

Same house, now a company. Buy an asset worth 100 with 100 of equity, and if it doubles you made 100% on your money. Buy it with 30 of equity and 70 of other people’s debt, and the same doubling leaves equity worth 130 on 30 in — more than four times, not two. That is why buyout funds lean on leverage. It is also why they pick **stable** businesses. The mortgage only works if the “house” does not collapse.

### Debt to assets

\[
\frac{\text{total debt}}{\text{total assets}}
\]

Share of the pile funded by debt.

### Debt to capitalization

\[
\frac{\text{debt}}{\text{debt} + \text{equity}}
\]

The ratio of long-term debt to **capitalization** (debt + equity) ignores operating liabilities (payables, accruals) and focuses on the two *financial* claims. Payables are real, but they are part of running the shop, not the capital-structure choice.

### Assets to equity (the magnification factor)

\[
\frac{\text{assets}}{\text{equity}}
\]

If this is 4, owners control four dollars of assets per dollar of their own capital. Banks can be 10. A cash-rich software firm can be close to 1.

This factor is the third piece of DuPont.

### Interest coverage

\[
\frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{interest expense}}
\]

From the *income statement*, not the balance sheet. Also called **times interest earned**. “Does this year’s operating profit cover the interest bill?”

A personal analogue: monthly income versus the mortgage payment.

- Around **1**: walking a tightrope; operations just cover interest
- Below **1**: operations do not even cover interest — emergency (Timberland, file 05)
- Comfortably above **5–10**: lenders sleep

A related hybrid is **debt / EBITDA** (stock of debt vs a year’s cash-ish earnings). It mixes the balance sheet with the income statement. Lenders love it.

### Who should borrow a lot? Pharma, LBOs, and risk

Stable cash, committed customers, low threat of sudden irrelevance.

**Reflection — drug companies 2001 to 2016:** large pharma slowly took on more leverage (Merck and Pfizer are the chapter’s pair). One reading: they started *buying* promising science from biotech instead of living through the riskiest years of discovery themselves. Cash flows got steadier. Lenders got more comfortable. Risk fell, so debt rose. That is the right direction of travel.

**Leveraged buyouts (LBOs):** a buyer loads the *company* with new debt to pay off old shareholders, leaving a much more levered firm. Good targets look boring on purpose: tobacco, gaming, utilities — committed customers, predictable demand, little substitution. A jumpy tech platform is a bad LBO.

Who should not borrow a lot: a chip firm that bets the company on a new plant every cycle. **Do not pile financial risk on top of business risk.** File 05 makes this Intel vs a power company.

## 4. Efficiency — squeezing the assets

### Asset turnover

\[
\text{Asset turnover} = \frac{\text{revenue}}{\text{total assets}}
\]

Sales generated per dollar of assets. Grocers are high. Utilities and software-with-huge-cash-piles can look low (the cash sits there, not selling cereal).

**Reflection — Walmart and 1990s productivity:** squeezing more sales (and lower costs) out of the same asset pile is not a slogan. Over long stretches it is the main engine of economic growth. Desai cites the McKinsey Global Institute view that Walmart, directly and by forcing rivals to copy it, drove a large share of US retail productivity gains in the 1990s. Those gains showed up less as fat pay packets and more as **cheaper goods**. Income inequality may not have fallen; **consumption** inequality did, because lower-income households buy more of what got cheaper.

“Productivity” in finance is narrower than in politics: more output from the asset pile. Inventory turns and asset turnover are how you see it on a page.

### Inventory turnover and days inventory

\[
\text{Inventory turnover} = \frac{\text{cost of goods sold}}{\text{inventory}}
\]

\[
\text{Days inventory} = \frac{365}{\text{inventory turnover}}
\]

How many times a year the shelf is emptied, and how many days a typical item sits.

| Business | Typical feeling |
|----------|-----------------|
| Fast food | inventory measured in days |
| Grocery | a couple of weeks |
| Bookstore | months |
| Pharma with long-dated product | can be slow |

Higher turnover is financially valuable because inventory is risky capital sitting still.

### Receivables collection period

How many days, on average, until customers pay.

\[
\text{Collection period (days)} \approx \frac{\text{receivables}}{\text{sales}} \times 365
\]

(Desai’s table prints this as a days figure. The idea is what matters.)

- Under ~15 days: mostly consumers paying now
- 30–90 days: selling to businesses
- Thousands of days: you are not looking at a shop. You are looking at a bank’s loan book.

Retail vs B2B is often the first cut in the matching game.

### Payable period (the third working-capital clock)

Inventory days and collection days are how cash gets **stuck**. Payable days are how long *you* take to pay suppliers.

\[
\text{Payable period (days)} \approx \frac{\text{accounts payable}}{\text{cost of goods sold}} \times 365
\]

The trio — inventory, receivables, payables — is **working capital**. Chapter 2 will make it a cash story. In Chapter 1 you already need it for Timberland: when a firm is dying, payable days can *fall*, because suppliers demand cash on delivery.

Chapter quiz flavour: **highest inventory turnover** among a sandwich chain, a bookstore, a grocer, and an airline? The sandwich chain. An airline has no merchandise inventory in the retail sense. A bookstore is slow. A grocer is fast, but food-on-a-plate is faster.

## Pocket card

| Family | Star ratios | Owner of the question |
|--------|-------------|------------------------|
| Liquidity | Current, quick | Suppliers, nervous CFOs |
| Profitability | Margin, ROE, ROA, EBITDA margin | Owners, and anyone comparing industries |
| Leverage | Debt/assets, assets/equity, EBIT/interest | Lenders, and owners who like (or fear) magnification |
| Efficiency | Asset turnover, inventory turns, collection days | Operators |

No single ratio is “the truth.” Each is a camera angle.

## Check yourself

1. Why might a grocer with a current ratio under 1 still be safe?
2. Two firms have the same ROE. One has fat margins and no debt. The other has thin margins and huge debt. Who is more fragile?
3. Why is the quick ratio stricter than the current ratio?
4. A company’s collection period is 8 days. Consumer or business customer?
5. Why might a luxury house care more about the quick ratio than a miner does?
6. What is “bad” about extra leverage if it raises ROE in good years?

**Answers:** (1) Inventory turns into cash in days, faster than supplier bills come due. (2) The levered thin-margin firm. (3) It refuses to count inventory as money you can certainly get. (4) Consumer. (5) Last season’s coats have no spot market; ore does. (6) Losses get magnified too; risk rises.

# 01 — The two statements

You only need two documents to start Chapter 1: the **balance sheet** and the **income statement**. (The cash-flow statement becomes the star of Chapter 2.)

← [Start here](00-start-here.md) · Next: [02 What the business owns and owes](02-what-the-business-owns-and-owes.md) →

---

## In one sentence

The balance sheet is a snapshot of what the company owns and how it paid for it. The income statement is a movie of how much came in and how much went out over a year.

---

## A personal picture first

Imagine your own finances on 31 December.

**Balance sheet (one day):**

| What you own | How you paid for it |
|--------------|---------------------|
| Cash in the bank | Credit-card bill still unpaid |
| Phone, laptop, clothes | Student loan |
| Maybe a car or a home | Mortgage |
| | **What’s left is your net worth** |

Your net worth is: stuff you own minus stuff you owe. Companies call that **shareholders’ equity**.

**Income statement (the whole year):**

Salary came in. Rent, food, and interest went out. What remains is savings. Companies call the leftover **net profit**.

If you reinvest those savings instead of spending them, your net worth grows. The same thing happens on a company’s books: leftover profit becomes **retained earnings**, which sit inside equity.

Desai’s household list is worth installing: clothes, washing machine, television, car, home on the left; credit cards, car loan, student loan, mortgage on the right. Human capital (your education) is the most valuable asset most people have, and it is almost invisible on a formal balance sheet — a preview of how companies also hide brands and patents.

---

## The golden identity

\[
\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Shareholders’ equity}
\]

This always holds. It is not a theory. It is how the books are built.

- **Assets:** what the company owns (cash, inventory, factories, patents it bought).
- **Liabilities:** what it owes to *lenders and suppliers* (not owners).
- **Equity:** the owners’ residual claim. Owners get whatever is left after everyone else is paid. They can also be wiped out first if the company fails.

Read it as: every rupee/dollar of stuff was funded either by creditors or by owners.

## Common-size: why everything is a percentage

A giant bank and a small restaurant cannot be compared in raw dollars. So analysts scale the balance sheet so **total assets = 100**. Every line becomes a share of the whole.

Starbucks in 2017, common-sized from its annual report (the example Desai walks), looked roughly like this. Totals round to 100.

| Assets (what it owns) | % | Funding (how it paid) | % |
|-----------------------|--:|-----------------------|--:|
| Cash | 19 | Accounts payable | 5 |
| Accounts receivable | 6 | Other current liabilities | 15 |
| Inventories | 9 | Long-term debt | 36 |
| Other current assets | 2 | Other liabilities | 5 |
| Property, plant, and equipment | 34 | **Shareholders’ equity** | **38** |
| Intangibles and other assets | 29 | | |
| **Total** | **100** | **Total** | **100** |

Read the shape, not the brand name: a **retailer with stores** (PP&E 34), real but modest inventory (coffee, cups, food — not unsold cars), a large intangible/acquisition pile (the brand and deals), and a financing mix that is both debt and equity. That is a coffee chain, not a bank and not a software firm sitting on cash.

The income statement is scaled the same way: **revenue = 100**. Starbucks 2017, again as presented in the chapter:

| Line | Per $100 of sales |
|------|------------------:|
| Revenue | 100 |
| Cost of goods sold | (40) |
| **Gross profit** | **60** |
| Selling, general, and administrative costs | (42) |
| **Operating profit (EBIT)** | **18** |
| Interest | (1) |
| Pretax income | 17 |
| Tax | (6) |
| **Net profit** | **11** |

Now “profit is $X billion” becomes “we keep 11 cents of every sales dollar.” That is comparable across years and rivals.

Same lesson with Coca-Cola, which Desai uses to kill raw numbers: billions of net profit is hard to judge; **about 16 cents kept per sales dollar** is a story. $64 billion of liabilities is hard to judge; **about 71% of assets funded by liabilities** is a story. Always attach a denominator.

The unidentified-industries table in the book is the same trick at scale: fourteen real 2013 firms, every balance-sheet line already a percentage of 100, plus a strip of ratios. Hundreds of cells look terrifying. Twenty-eight of them are just the “100” totals. Ignore the fog. Hunt the extremes.

## Walk down the income statement

Keep these four profit ideas separate. They answer different questions.

| Name | What was subtracted | Question it answers |
|------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Gross profit** | Direct cost of making/buying the goods | How juicy is the product itself? |
| **Operating profit / EBIT** | Also rent, salaries, marketing, depreciation | How good is the *business*, ignoring how it is financed and taxed? |
| **Net profit** | Also interest and tax | What is left for owners? |
| **EBITDA** | EBIT with depreciation and amortization added back | Rough cash generated by operations, before interest and tax |

**EBIT** = earnings before interest and taxes. It is just operating profit with a finance nickname.

**EBITDA** = EBIT + depreciation + amortization. Pronounced “eh-bit-dah.” Depreciation is an accounting charge for factories wearing out; it is not cash leaving the building this year. Adding it back is a step toward cash. Chapter 2 will be obsessed with this.

**Beginner trap:** a company can show a net *loss* and still throw off a lot of EBITDA. Amazon in the mid-2010s is the famous example: accounting profit near zero, cash generation from operations much larger, because depreciation was huge. Chapter 1 already flags this; Chapter 2 puts numbers on it.

**Why EBIT exists:** net profit is after interest and tax. An American publisher and a German publisher can run identical shops and still show different net profit because tax codes differ. One firm can also choose more debt than the other. EBIT (operating profit) lets you compare the *shops*, not the tax lawyers and the capital structure.

## Current vs non-current

On both sides of the balance sheet, items are ordered by time.

**Assets** are ordered by how quickly they can become cash:

1. Cash and marketable securities (tomorrow)
2. Receivables (weeks to months — customers who have not paid yet)
3. Inventory (must be sold first)
4. PP&E (factories, planes, stores — years)
5. Other / intangibles (brands, patents, goodwill — fuzzy)

Items 1–3 plus a small bucket of **other current assets** (prepayments, odds and ends due within a year) are **current assets**: expected to turn into cash within a year.

If all your wealth is a house, you are **illiquid**. If it is in a checking account, you are highly **liquid**. Companies are the same. Liquidity is not the same as wealth.

**Liabilities** are ordered by how soon they must be paid:

1. Notes payable, accounts payable, accruals (soon)
2. Long-term debt and pensions (years)

Items due within a year are **current liabilities**.

This ordering is not decoration. Liquidity analysis is literally “can the top of the left side cover the top of the right side?”

## Finance vs accounting, already

Chapter 1 is not a bookkeeping class. No debits, no credits.

Two accounting habits already matter:

1. **Conservatism.** If accountants cannot measure an asset precisely, they often record it at zero. Coca-Cola’s brand may be the most valuable thing it owns. It still may not appear as an asset until someone *buys* the company and a price is paid.
2. **Historical cost.** Buildings sit at what was paid years ago, not what they would sell for today.

That is why finance people treat the balance sheet as a useful sketch, not a photograph of economic reality. Ratios still work, because you are comparing shapes, not claiming the numbers are “true value.” Conservatism is also why a brand *built* inside the firm can be worth a fortune and still show as zero, while the same brand, once *purchased*, suddenly appears. File 02 takes that all the way to goodwill.

## Check yourself

1. If assets are 100, liabilities are 70, what is equity?
2. Why might two companies with the same net profit not be equally healthy?
3. Which profit number ignores financing and tax: gross profit, EBIT, or net profit?
4. Why would an analyst scale a balance sheet so assets equal 100?
5. Why might two publishers with identical operations show different *net* profit?

**Answers:** (1) 30. (2) One might need far more assets or far more debt to earn that profit; ratios catch that. (3) EBIT. (4) So you can compare the *mix* of a small firm and a giant firm. (5) Tax rates and interest (capital structure) sit below EBIT.

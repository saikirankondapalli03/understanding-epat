## What this chapter is trying to do to you

Most people are taught to *read* financial statements. Desai wants you to *see through* them.

A number by itself is almost never useful. “Profit was $100 million” could mean a triumph or a disaster. You only know after you compare it to something: sales, assets, equity, last year, or a rival in the same industry.

That comparison is a **ratio**. Ratios are the language of finance. Chapter 1 teaches you to speak it.

Financial analysis answers questions that CFOs, managers, investors, and bankers actually ask:

- How **safe** is it to lend to this company?
- How **rewarding** is it to be a shareholder?
- How much **value** does this company provide?
- Will it still be here in six to twelve months?

No one line on a statement can answer those. Ratios can, once you have a story.

## Complete Chapter 1 coverage checklist

Tick these as you go. If a row is blank in your head, you are not done.

### A. What financial analysis is

- [ ] Analysis is intuition around ratios, not debits and credits
- [ ] A raw number (profit, liabilities, cash) is meaningless without a comparison
- [ ] Common-size statements: assets = 100, revenue = 100
- [ ] First move in a sea of numbers: hunt **extremes**, then invent a story, then confirm
- [ ] Three audiences: lenders (safety), owners (return), managers (value and viability)

### B. The two statements

- [ ] Balance sheet = snapshot of what you own and how you paid for it
- [ ] Income statement = year’s movie from revenue to net profit
- [ ] Assets = liabilities + shareholders’ equity (always)
- [ ] Personal analogy: clothes/car/home vs credit cards/student loans/mortgage; leftover is net worth
- [ ] Starbucks 2017 as a common-size example
- [ ] Current vs non-current (time ordering on both sides)
- [ ] Conservatism and historical cost — why finance distrusts a literal reading of accounts

### C. Assets (what the firm is)

- [ ] Häagen-Dazs picture: ice cream, factory, trucks
- [ ] Cash and marketable securities; why firms hold mountains of cash (insurance, war chest, no projects)
- [ ] Apple-scale cash; cash is usually parked in government securities, not a checking account
- [ ] Accounts receivable; 30 / 60 / 90 day trade credit
- [ ] B2C vs B2B receivables (Walmart vs Staples vs Intel)
- [ ] Inventory: raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods
- [ ] Zero inventory → service firm
- [ ] PP&E: stores, dams, planes, factories — tools, not merchandise
- [ ] Other assets / intangibles / brands / patents
- [ ] Goodwill: purchase price minus book value (Microsoft–LinkedIn)
- [ ] Internally built patents often missing (Gilead-style example)

### D. Liabilities and equity (who funded it)

- [ ] Only two ultimate sources: lenders and owners = **capital structure**
- [ ] Accounts payable = someone else’s receivable (Intel ↔ Dell / Lenovo)
- [ ] High payables: distress *or* power over suppliers — check cash
- [ ] Notes payable: short-term financial debt; banks live here
- [ ] Accrued items (wages mid-cycle)
- [ ] Long-term debt has an **explicit interest rate**; payables usually do not (Home Depot bonds vs supplier bills)
- [ ] Cash as negative debt / **net debt**
- [ ] Equity, retained earnings, residual claim, last in bankruptcy
- [ ] Preferred stock as a hybrid; why VCs want it; why a shaky firm issues it; Buffett / Dow
- [ ] Synonyms: net worth, owners’ equity, common stock

### E. Ratio families

- [ ] Liquidity: most bankruptcies are cash deaths
- [ ] Current ratio (suppliers’ question; 6–12 month survival)
- [ ] Quick ratio, including the book’s version (cash + securities + receivables) / current liabilities
- [ ] Inventory is risky: BlackBerry Z10 write-down; Burberry vs Rio Tinto vs Nucor
- [ ] Liquidity–return trade-off (cash is safe and lazy)
- [ ] Profit margin, gross vs operating vs net
- [ ] EBIT (compare across tax regimes: US vs German publisher)
- [ ] EBITDA and EBITDA margin (utilities, Amazon, depreciation)
- [ ] ROE, ROA
- [ ] House-leverage story; you are not richer in the bigger mortgaged house
- [ ] Private equity as the same story (Alan Jones)
- [ ] Debt / assets, debt / capitalization, assets / equity
- [ ] Interest coverage / times interest earned; debt / EBITDA
- [ ] Pharma leverage 2001–2016; LBO targets (sticky demand)
- [ ] Asset turnover; inventory turnover; days inventory
- [ ] Receivables collection period; payable period
- [ ] Walmart / 1990s productivity and cheaper consumer prices
- [ ] Working capital trio: inventory, receivables, payables

## The one idea to carry around

> Look at extremes. Invent a story. Then check the story against a second number.

That is financial analysis. Everything else is vocabulary.

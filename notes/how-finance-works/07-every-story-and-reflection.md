## How the chapter is built

Desai does not lecture and then give examples. He gives a **game**, then **Reflections** (pause-and-think boxes), then **Real-World Perspectives** (a CFO or investor talking), then an extended time-series case (Timberland). This file collects every one of those so none is left as a throwaway.

## Every Reflection in the chapter (restated)

The book’s pause-boxes, in order of appearance, with the answer Desai is steering you toward.

1. **Walmart, Staples, Intel — who has more receivables relative to sales?**  
   Intel (B2B). Walmart tiny. Staples in between.

2. **Microsoft–LinkedIn: what was the extra $19B for?**  
   Unrecorded intangibles: member data, network, a way to sell more software.

3. **Those three firms’ receivables are whose payables?**  
   Intel → PC makers (Dell, Lenovo). Always name the customer.

4. **Company E vs I: whose long-term debt is riskier?**  
   Look at cash. Cash is negative debt. I is riskier on a net basis.

5. **Why do VCs take preferred?**  
   Downside paid before common; convert and ride the upside if it works.

6. **Rio Tinto, Nucor, Burberry — current or quick ratio?**  
   Burberry: quick (no spot market for fashion). Rio: current is less misleading. Nucor in between.

7. **Why did pharma leverage rise? What makes a good LBO?**  
   Steadier cash as big pharma bought biotech science. LBOs want committed, substitutable-slowly cash: tobacco, gaming, utilities.

8. **Where did Walmart-era productivity show up?**  
   Not only wages. Lower consumer prices; consumption inequality eased even if income inequality did not.

Timberland’s “why is collection so long?” is the same species of reflection: slack management, promotional credit, or hidden bad debts. All three.

## Real-World Perspectives

**Alan Jones** (Morgan Stanley, private equity): the house-mortgage analogy is how buyouts work. Other people’s money turns a double into more than a four-bagger. That is the attraction and the danger.

**Laurence Debroux** (Heineken CFO): after a point, grind and technical skill are not the scarce trait. **Persistence** (the first number is not the last) and **curiosity** (what people actually do, what the assumption was) are. Numbers are dry until they are a reality.

## Chapter summary, in Desai’s own closing key

Financial analysis is more than numbers. Numbers are tools to understand performance **across time, across companies, and across industries**. Each ratio helps; none is the whole story; each has limits. Piecing them into a narrative is the work. It gets easier with hours. The exam of Chapter 1 is not a formula sheet. It is: **can you walk someone else through the unidentified-industries game, then do a favourite company yourself?**

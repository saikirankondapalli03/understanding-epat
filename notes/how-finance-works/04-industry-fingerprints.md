## In one sentence

A business’s economics leave a silhouette on the balance sheet. Learn the silhouettes, then confirm with a second ratio.

Play the game **in the book** before you read anyone’s answers, including these notes. Struggle is the point.

## The 14 businesses in the game

Desai uses 2013 figures for fourteen well-known firms, unlabeled. The industries are:

| Kind of animal | Examples in the game |
|----------------|----------------------|
| Consumer retail | Amazon, Barnes & Noble, Kroger, Walgreens, Yum! |
| Retail with its own credit card | Nordstrom |
| Software / internet | Microsoft, Facebook |
| Hardware on a lean model | Dell |
| Science + patents | Pfizer |
| Heavy networks | Duke Energy, UPS, Southwest |
| Bank | Citigroup |

Your job is not to memorize 2013 numbers. Your job is to build a **decision tree**.

## Decision tree (use this every time)

```
1. Inventory = 0?
      yes → service (bank, airline, parcel, social network…)
      no  → sells goods

2. If service:
      receivables enormous + notes payable huge → bank
      PP&E huge → planes / trucks / networks (airline vs parcel)
      cash mountain + fat equity, little PP&E → young internet platform

3. If sells goods, collection period tiny (~under 2 weeks)?
      yes → true retailer (consumer pays now)
      no  → B2B, or a retailer that *is* a mini-bank via its own credit card

4. Among retailers, sort by inventory speed:
      days    → restaurant
      weeks   → grocery / drugstore (then use PP&E: grocers have more cold chain)
      months  → books / slow goods
      low PP&E + high payables + thin/negative profit → giant online retailer

5. Among the leftovers:
      PP&E huge + almost no inventory + fat EBITDA margin → utility
      PP&E real + inventory real + slower collections → department store with a charge card
      almost no PP&E + fat margins → software or pharma
      almost no PP&E + thin margins + lightning inventory turns → lean PC assembler
      fat “other assets” + pensions → acquisitive pharma
      fat cash, fat margins, not much “other” → software giant sitting on a war chest
```

That tree is the chapter.

## Fingerprints worth installing in your head

### Bank

The confusing one. Remember the mirror:

| On *your* books | On the *bank’s* books |
|-----------------|------------------------|
| Mortgage (your debt) | Loan (its asset / “receivable”) |
| Deposit (your cash) | Notes payable / deposits (its liability) |

So a bank shows:

- receivables that look insane (years, not days)
- little or no inventory or PP&E
- very high leverage
- a “spread” business: charge more on loans than you pay on deposits

Banks take short-term money and lend it long-term. That maturity mismatch plus leverage is why they are valuable and why they sometimes explode.

### Airline vs parcel carrier

Both: service, no inventory, huge PP&E (planes, hubs, fleets).

Separate them with **who pays**:

- Airline tickets: consumers, cash-like, collection in a few days
- Parcel logistics: other companies, invoices, slower collection

A second clue: an old, unionized parcel firm may carry large **pension** liabilities (“other liabilities”). A younger budget airline often does not.

### Young social network

Service, no inventory, not much PP&E. After an IPO: **cash mountain** and **equity mountain**. Later, if it buys other apps for huge prices, cash falls and goodwill (“other assets”) rises.

### Restaurant vs grocer vs drugstore vs bookstore vs online giant

All collect from consumers fast. Sort by **inventory** and **stores**:

| Clue | Likely |
|------|--------|
| Inventory gone in ~10 days | Restaurant |
| Fast turns + lots of equipment (refrigeration) | Grocery |
| Fast-ish turns, less heavy plant, some insurance receivables | Drugstore |
| Inventory that sleeps for months, maybe losses, maybe preferred stock | Bookstore in the Amazon era |
| Less PP&E than a supermarket, huge payables, lots of cash, profit ~0 | Online retailer using supplier credit |

Amazon in that era was famous for: not showing much net profit, holding cash, and taking its time paying suppliers. High payables + high cash is power, not panic.

### Utility vs department store

Both can show big PP&E.

- Utility: electricity cannot sit in a warehouse → **almost no inventory**, **high depreciation** → EBITDA margin much fatter than net margin, decent leverage because cash flows are regulated and stable
- Department store: **real inventory** (clothes), and if it runs its **own charge card**, receivables linger like a bank’s

### Software vs pharma vs lean PC maker

All can show little PP&E.

| | Software | Pharma | Lean PC assembler |
|--|----------|--------|-------------------|
| Margins | very high | very high | thin (the product became a commodity) |
| Cash | often huge | depends | moderate |
| Other assets | some | often huge (years of buying other drug firms) | some |
| Inventory | near zero | some | tiny and *fast* (build after the order) |
| Extra clue | young-ish pensions | old defined-benefit pensions | receivables from business customers |

Dell’s historical trick: do not build the machine until the customer orders it. Inventory days collapse. Margins still look ordinary because PCs are a tough, crowded market.

## B2B vs B2C, one more time

This single idea unlocks half the game.

- **B2C** (business to consumer): collection period short
- **B2B** (business to business): collection period looks like 30–90 days

Nordstrom’s private-label card is the exception that teaches the rule: a consumer brand can *choose* to become a bit of a bank.

Intel-like firms sell to Dell-like firms. The chipmaker’s receivable *is* the PC maker’s payable.

## How to practice without spoiling the book

1. Open Table 1-1 in the book. Cover the later pages that solve it.
2. Highlight only extremes (zeros, 60%+ PP&E, 50%+ cash, negative margins).
3. Split columns into service vs goods.
4. Split goods into fast-pay vs slow-pay.
5. Only then hunt the famous individual firms.
6. After you have a full matching, read Desai’s walkthrough and argue with it.

Teaching someone else the game is the test of whether you actually learned it.

## Check yourself

1. Zero inventory, 80%+ of assets in “loans / receivables,” huge short-term funding. What is it?
2. Why is an airline a service firm even though it owns billions in aircraft?
3. Two high-margin, low-PP&E firms remain. One has a mountain of “other assets.” Which industry is more likely?
4. Why might a grocer have more PP&E than a drugstore?

**Answers:** (1) A bank. (2) It sells seats, not planes; aircraft are tools, not merchandise. (3) An acquisitive pharma (or any serial buyer of intangible businesses). (4) Cold chain: refrigeration, bigger stores, more equipment to move perishable food.
